How to Fund a Liquidity Pool Across Chains

Funding a pool across chains means getting the right tokens onto the pool’s network, then depositing them; bridges add cost, delay and separate risks.

Onchain Report NewsroomOct 17, 2026 · 02:51 UTC#4cadeb3 min read

To fund a liquidity pool across chains, first get the pool’s required tokens onto the blockchain where it lives, then deposit them through the pool’s own interface. The two steps can involve different protocols: a bridge moves tokens between networks, while a decentralized exchange pool uses them to enable trades. Knowing where the pool sits and what it accepts helps prevent a costly wrong turn.

What does funding a pool across chains involve?

It means moving funds to the pool’s network before you add them, unless the service offers a cross-chain deposit flow. For example, if a pool is on BNB Chain and your tokens are on Solana, you need a supported route that delivers the right assets to BNB Chain. A bridge is a service that transfers value between blockchains, often by locking or pooling assets on one side and releasing tokens on the other.

The route can include a swap as well as a bridge. A guide to using Biswap to move Solana assets to BNB Chain covers that kind of chain-to-chain journey in more detail. Before sending, check that the destination token is accepted by the pool you intend to fund; a token with a familiar name may still be a different asset or version.

What do you need before depositing?

Check the pool’s network, token pair and deposit rules. Many pools require both assets in a pair, such as a stablecoin and another token. Some ask for roughly equal value in each; others use a different ratio or let you deposit one asset and swap part of it. The pool’s interface should show the required amounts and any approval steps.

  • Confirm the exact network and token versions.
  • Keep enough of that network’s native token for transaction fees.
  • Check bridge fees, the estimated arrival amount and the receiving address.
  • Review the pool’s deposit ratio and how withdrawals work.

After the transfer arrives, connect the wallet to the pool on the destination network. Approve token use if asked, then submit the deposit. Start with an amount you can afford to have tied up while you learn how that pool works.

What are the trade-offs and risks?

Moving funds first gives you direct control over the destination assets, but it adds steps, fees and waiting time. A cross-chain deposit flow may bundle those steps into one request, but the service still relies on a route that must deliver the right assets. Check what happens if a transfer is delayed or cannot complete.

Pool deposits also carry risks separate from the bridge. Token prices can move while your assets are in the pool, changing their value compared with simply holding them. This is often called impermanent loss: a difference in value caused by price changes while you provide liquidity. Fees from trades may offset some of that change, but they are not guaranteed to do so.

The practical rule is simple: identify the pool’s chain and assets first, then choose a transfer route that lands those assets there. Treat the bridge and the pool as two separate decisions, and check each one before committing funds.
