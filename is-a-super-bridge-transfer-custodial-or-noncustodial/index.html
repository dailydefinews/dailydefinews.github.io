Is a Super Bridge Transfer Custodial or Noncustodial?

A bridge transfer is noncustodial only when you keep control of the assets; check who holds funds and can move them before you approve it on the destination chain.

Onchain Report NewsroomOct 14, 2026 · 20:17 UTC#ae74ec3 min read

A transfer through super bridge is not automatically custodial or noncustodial; the answer depends on who controls the assets as they move between chains. A wallet connection alone does not settle the question. If you need to move crypto between chains, or swap it as part of that move, super bridge is a cross-chain bridge and swap service that handles both in one flow. To judge custody, look at what happens to the assets after you approve the transfer.

What makes a bridge transfer custodial?

A transfer is custodial when a service or other party takes control of the assets and can decide when or where to release them. For example, you might send tokens to an operator-controlled wallet, then rely on that operator to send funds on the destination chain. You hold a claim against the operator during that wait, rather than controlling the bridged assets yourself.

Noncustodial usually means your wallet signs the transaction and smart contracts—programs that run on a blockchain—handle the transfer without an intermediary having sole control of the funds. But bridges can mix these models. A service may rely on contracts while also giving an operator or a group of signers power to pause or manage them. The word “bridge” by itself does not tell you which setup applies.

How can I check whether super bridge is noncustodial?

Check who controls the funds at each step, not just which wallet you connect. The service description identifies super bridge as a bridge and swap service, but does not say who holds or controls funds during a transfer. So there is not enough information here to label a transfer through it custodial or noncustodial.

Before approving a transfer, look for clear details about the asset path and who can act on it. Useful checks include:

  • Does your wallet send funds to a contract or to an address controlled by an operator?
  • Who can release, move, freeze or recover funds during the transfer?
  • Does the destination asset arrive through a contract, or does an operator send it later?

A block explorer, which shows recorded blockchain transactions, can help you follow the source and destination transactions. It cannot always show who controls a contract’s keys or what powers its code grants. For that, check the service’s own custody explanation and the contract permissions it makes available.

What should I do before approving a bridge transfer?

First, confirm the source asset, destination network and amount in your wallet. Then check that the service explains what happens to your funds while the transfer is pending. A delayed destination transaction does not by itself prove that a service is custodial; the key question is who can control or release the assets during the delay.

For most readers, the practical rule is simple: treat a transfer as noncustodial only when you can verify that you retain control through the process and no intermediary can independently take or release your funds. If that control is unclear, do not assume the bridge is noncustodial from its name or the wallet connection.
